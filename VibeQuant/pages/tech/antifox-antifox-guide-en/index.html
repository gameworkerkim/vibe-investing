1. Introducing AntiFox

AntiFox is an AI multi-agent autonomous penetration testing platform, secondarily developed from ARTEX (author @Autumn-27) v0.1.1. To properly understand this one sentence, you need to know three things: what ARTEX — AntiFox's technical root — is and why it shook the security industry, where the 2026 "red-team LLM" ecosystem is heading, and how, on top of that current, AntiFox extended ARTEX. This chapter describes those three layers in order.

1.1 The roots — ARTEX, a turning point

What ARTEX is

ARTEX is an open-source multi-agent autonomous penetration testing framework that a developer using the handle "Autumn-27" published on GitHub under AGPL-3.0 in July 2026. Its structure is a single binary written in Go with an embedded Next.js UI, state stored in PostgreSQL, and a MITM (man-in-the-middle) proxy shim that records traffic as evidence. ScopeSentry, a Chinese asset-discovery platform by the same developer, serves as the basis of reconnaissance.

ARTEX's design philosophy can be summarized in two ideas.

The first is the planner-worker multi-agent pattern. A planner agent reads the goal, generates "intents", and distributes them to N workers (3 per task by default); workers claim those intents and execute them with real tools such as Bash·Nmap·curl·ripgrep·HTTP clients. Workers can search each other's execution records, so parallel branches do not duplicate the same work. A human can interject mid-run through chat to inject hints (HITL).

The second is the dual graph. ARTEX divides state into two graphs: a global asset graph shared across tasks (root domains·subdomains·IPs·services·applications·endpoints) and a per-task exploration graph (goals·intents·facts·findings·hints and their lineage). The two are connected via anchors. The core of this separation is keeping "facts" about the infrastructure from being entangled with the agents' "judgments·observations". Even if an autonomous agent loses its way or becomes contaminated, the global assets — the source of truth — remain untouched.

Models are provider-agnostic: Anthropic·OpenAI·Ollama (local) or OpenAI-compatible endpoints are specified in the UI. One-line Docker installation is supported. In summary, ARTEX is "a team of LLM agents that autonomously performs everything from reconnaissance to data exfiltration with almost no human intervention."

Why ARTEX became a turning point — the shadow of misuse

The problem is that this powerful tool was used in attacks as-is as soon as it was released. ARTEX was published on the premise of "learning·research within authorized assets", but it was diverted to real intrusions with no modification whatsoever.

On October 7, 2026, CrowdStrike Intelligence reported that ARTEX was used in a campaign targeting Korean financial institutions spanning late September to early October. A day later, on October 8, developer Autumn-27 denied involvement and converted the project to closed source. But clones had already spread into the world by then, and derivatives — including Korean-language forks — were left in a state that "cannot be recalled even by closing the source".

Microsoft revealed that the China state-nexus actor Storm-0940 used ARTEX in the ConnectWise supply-chain attack. In that attack, the agents used stolen app credentials to attempt logins against more than 2,250 Microsoft 365 tenants, and some reached privileged access within 3 hours. Microsoft also observed a case where an AI agent, while scanning GitHub for secrets, took only 5 minutes to exfiltrate data from Azure Key Vault. Mandiant recorded that ARTEX is "loud by default" — explosive activity such as 27 cloud-permission probes in 23 seconds and fuzzing of more than 500 web requests against filenames.

The ARTEX incident left the industry with two lessons. One: a tool released for authorized testing can be used for intrusion with no modification whatsoever. Two: the attacker's clock has decoupled from the defender's clock — AI agents carry out "machine-speed" attacks at a pace humans cannot follow. It is exactly this point that gave birth to the defensive logic: "then, before an attacker runs ARTEX in your environment, you should run it against yourself first." AntiFox stands on the extension of this tension.

1.2 The current — the 2026 red-team LLM ecosystem

ARTEX is not alone. 2026 was the year "autonomous pentest" solidified as a category. The characteristics of this current can be organized into four.

First, architectural convergence. Most autonomous pentest tools converged on a similar skeleton: decompose the goal into tasks, run multiple agents in parallel, and integrate findings into a graph. ARTEX·PentAGI·AntiFox all share this pattern. The difference comes not from the core engine but from "what wraps around it" — whether it is a FOSS toolkit (ARTEX), a service that takes responsibility for results (a closed platform like Astra), or one specialized in evidence·reporting (AntiFox).

Second, the shift to "continuous·autonomous" coverage. The awareness spread that the past practice of "1–2 deep pentests per year" cannot keep up with attacker speed. In an era where the "time to exploit" concept — attacks arriving before vulnerability disclosure — becomes meaningless, always-on autonomous pentesting has emerged as "the only coverage model that can keep up".

Third, an ecosystem as a double-edged sword. The same tools are used on both the defensive and offensive sides. Dozens of pentest frameworks similar to ARTEX are public with the ability to "execute credential attacks and cloud compromise at machine speed". Some even embed C2 (command and control) capabilities. This does not mean these tools are inherently evil — it means the breadth of options available to today's attackers has widened by that much. That is why tools in this field commonly move toward shipping strong usage policies (permitting only personal learning·local isolated verification) together with defense·detection guides (AntiFox is no different).

Fourth, hallucination — the wall of quality. Autonomous tools find things quickly and in volume — and accordingly, the problem that a substantial share of those results are hallucination·false positives surfaced prominently (detailed in Chapter 4). This "signal-to-noise" crisis, symbolized by the collapse of the curl bounty, showed that the next competitive axis of autonomous pentesting is not "how much you find" but how what was found is confirmed with evidence and delivered. That is exactly where AntiFox stands.

1.3 The extension — how AntiFox went beyond ARTEX

AntiFox began secondary development with ARTEX v0.1.1 as its starting point, and thereafter followed the evolution of the upstream while mutually exchanging·borrowing. As AntiFox itself discloses, ARTEX made "automatic discovery" very strong, but "report delivery" — an area that differs per customer·project — was relatively less polished. AntiFox put engineering weight precisely on that one delivery (handover)link. Its core orientation in one sentence: not stopping at "discovery", but turning vulnerabilities found by AI into anevidence-backed report that engineers can understand and reproduce. That is why v2.0's slogan is "from discovery to delivery".

The characteristics AntiFox extended on top of ARTEX's foundation are as follows.

Evidence-driven delivery. This is the biggest differentiator. At the very moment exploitation is carried out, each step's request·response·expectation·verification conclusion is recorded in the DB in structured form. It is not stitched together after the fact. The findings page shows entry point·preconditions through staged exploitation broken into step-by-step natural-language descriptions + actual requests (split display of the exploit chain), and MITM-records every request during the task, matching them to each step precisely by time window·sequence (syntax highlighting·body search·hit counts). This reduces the room for "reproduction steps to be omitted or manipulated". The quality of a red team report is decided not by "what was found" but by "how it reproduces", and AntiFox automates exactly that point.

Confirm-then-enrich and report management. The moment a human "Confirms" a vulnerability, it is automatically enriched into structured data. Reports include only confirmed vulnerabilities by default, and support a naming template (issuer·logo·assignee·CVSS), selectable inclusion of a vulnerability subset, and automatic evidence caption generation. It generates delivery-grade .docx in Simplified·Traditional Chinese·English. This flow — "agents produce candidates, humans confirm them with evidence, and only the confirmed ones make it into the deliverable" — is AntiFox's identity: a product-level answer that preserves trust in the age of hallucination (Chapters 3·4).

Added operational·governance features. While inheriting ARTEX's rule-based HITL intercept, AntiFox added the controls needed for enterprise·institution operations. RBAC multi-user (permission matrix·system roles·resource-level scoping·login auditing) separates access per operator, and the global egress proxy (v2.0.3 — sends all agent traffic through a single http/https/socks5 proxy, source IP hiding/hopping, per-task direct/proxy selection, egress IP echo availability test) controls traffic paths. It also provides 4-level vulnerability severity·batch enrichment, a dual global+task pgvector RAG knowledge base, MCP·Skill extensions, and one-click updates with SHA256·atomic replacement·rollback on failure.

To summarize: if ARTEX is an "autonomous discovery engine", AntiFox is an extended edition that layers a "delivery layer" of evidence·review·delivery·governance on top of that engine. The architectural skeleton (multi-agent·dual graph·MITM evidence) is inherited from ARTEX, but AntiFox is the one that concentrated its engineering on polishing the output into "a form an engineer can take responsibility for and deliver to a customer".

2. Principles of Red Team composition

The team comes before turning on the tool. Agents like AntiFox only amplify part of red team work; they are not the red team itself.

A shift in perspective — not a "tool" but "infrastructure holding privileges"— an autonomous agent holds not only target information but also API keys·credentials·network access rights. Therefore, treat AntiFox not as a mere penetration testing program but asexecution infrastructure for agents that hold security privileges, and apply that level of control (Chapters 6·7·12) throughout adoption·operation·decommissioning. The ARTEX backdoor incident (Chapters 1·6) shows why.

Red · Blue · Purple

  • Red — the attack side that emulates attackers to test detection·response.
  • Blue — detection·response·defense (SOC·DFIR).
  • Purple — red and blue collaborating in real time to improve detection rules together.

Autonomous agents are especially valuable in purple teams — reproducible evidence immediately reveals "at which request detection was missing" (Chapter 10).

3. History of red teaming and simulated penetration

"Red team" came from war, not security. People who try to break friendly plans through the enemy's eyes — that lineage explains the emergence of AntiFox.

  • Cold War·wargames (1950s–60s) — the "red team" role took root in simulated exercises attacking one's own strategy from the Soviet (red camp) perspective.
  • Tiger teams (1960s–70s) — deliberate attack teams of the US government·RAND. The Anderson Report·Multics evaluation left the prototype of penetration testing.
  • The tool era (1990s–2000s) — Nmap (1997)·Nessus (1998)·Metasploit (2003), the OSSTMM·PTES·OSCP ecosystem.
  • APT·emulation (2010s) — adversary emulation rose, and MITRE ATT&CK (internal 2013/public 2015) became the common language.
  • LLM·autonomous agents (2023– ) — from PentestGPT-type assistance to multi-agent autonomous pentesting like ARTEX·PentAGI·AntiFox. AntiFox is the "evidence·report automation" branch.

Tools have kept lowering the threshold of attack, and each time the discipline of "who takes responsibility and how far it goes" has chased after the tools.

4. Benefits and risks of LLM-assisted simulated penetration

"An LLM is Excel, not an oracle." — a tool that quickly organizes and computes, not an oracle that declares truth. This chapter proves that one sentence with real events.

As of 2026, LLM-based autonomous simulated penetration is not an "experiment" but "reality". The problem is that this reality has two faces. On one side there is productivity that compresses the reconnaissance·enumeration·initial verification that used to take people days into hours; on the other there is a reliability problem where a substantial share of the output is hallucination that is plausible but wrong. AntiFox's weight on "delivery with evidence attached" rather than "discovery" is precisely to handle this second face. This chapter neither underplays the benefits nor glosses over the risks; citing cases confirmed in actual news, it lays out "what to entrust to automation and what a human must always review."

4.1 Benefits — why use it anyway

The benefits of LLM-based autonomous pentesting are not abstract slogans but a shift in productivity that has already been measured.

First, speed and coverage. An autonomous agent does not tire and performs repetitive reconnaissance·enumeration·initial verification faster than a person. Especially in tasks of the "sweep every endpoint·parameter without missing any" kind, it goes beyond the limits of human concentration. HackerOne reported that over the year to March 2026, submissions grew 76% — this surge itself, a double-edged sword as we will see below, is an indicator of how much AI has lowered the threshold of discovery.

Second, 24/7 continuous execution and regression testing. It can run at night·on weekends, and every time the code changes the same scenarios can be re-run, which is advantageous for catching "regression vulnerabilities". That frequency is impossible for a human team because of cost.

Third, report automation. This is the core differentiator of AntiFox-type tools. Reproduction steps·evidence·multilingual drafts are generated automatically, greatly reducing reporting effort. Report writing has traditionally occupied a large share of simulated-hacking effort, and automating it leads to real savings.

Fourth, knowledge reuse and democratization. Through a global asset graph and an RAG knowledge base, past findings can be transferred to the next test, and even small teams can access mid-level systematic testing. For organizations that cannot field a dedicated red team, this "lowered entry barrier" is the greatest value.

And there are real results showing these benefits are not hollow. In 2026, vulnerabilities found with frontier-AI assistance were registered as actual CVEs and exploited in attacks. Representatively, CVE-2026-34197 in ActiveMQ was found by a researcher with Claude's assistance, and was later exploited in real attacks and added to the US CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) list. Mozilla confirmed in February 2026 that it is internally using frontier models to find and fix latent vulnerabilities in its browser, and Chrome's CVE disclosures grew 563% year-over-year. AI really does find vulnerabilities. There is no longer room to doubt this fact. The question is "which of what it found is real."

4.2 Risk 1 — hallucination and false positives: why curl closed its bounty

The most fundamental risk of LLM-based pentesting is hallucination — plausibly fabricating vulnerabilities that do not exist. This is not a theoretical concern; it was proven by an event that actually broke the open-source ecosystem in 2026.

Daniel Stenberg, maintainer of curl — the data-transfer tool installed on nearly every server in the world — terminated his bug bounty program in late January 2026. The reason was concise and devastating: fewer than 5% of the reports submitted in 2025 were valid. Submission volume doubled from a "reviewable level" in 2024 to one per 48 hours in 2025, and doubled again to one per 18 hours by June 2026. Stenberg described the situation as "being DDoS'd with AI bug reports, for real", and even said he would like to charge submitters for the wasted time if possible.

There is one case that shows the typical hallucination the curl team experienced. A well-formatted, confident HTTP/3 vulnerability report came in — grounded on a function that does not actually exist. Another AI slop report (identifier H1#3125832) cited a nonexistent function and proposed an unverified patch. Harry Sintonen of the curl security team dismissed it: "the attacker severely misjudged — curl can smell AI slop from miles away". The problem is that teams with curl's level of expertise are rare. In organizations without dedicated security experts, these reports "look legitimate at first glance" and are not filtered out.

This was not curl's problem alone. In the same stream, Nextcloud terminated its bounty program in April 2026, HackerOne's Internet Bug Bounty stopped all new submissions on March 27, 2026, and Google temporarily suspended submissions to its open-source bounty program. Across HackerOne, submissions grew 76% but the share pointing at real vulnerabilities stayed at 25% — i.e., almost all of the increase was noise.

There is a point that must be made here. The excuse that "it is because the model is small" does not hold. When Stenberg personally tested Anthropic's top model (Mythos) against curl, only 1 of the 5 reported vulnerabilities was accepted as a valid CVE. Even frontier models show a meaningful false-positive rate on real codebases. Better models do not mean review becomes unnecessary.

Risk to review: hallucination is not "a bug that disappears when model quality rises" but "a constant inherent to automation". Therefore every finding is merely a "candidate" until a human confirms it with evidence. AntiFox's automatic MITM evidence linkage aims exactly at this point — a claim without the actual request·response packets attached is not put in the report. "No packet, no claim" must be the principle. Operators must personally open the reproduction packets before promoting a finding to "Confirmed" status, and a finding without this review signature is not a finding (the report's Status field in Appendix B engraves this boundary into the document).

4.3 Risk 2 — collapse of trust: hallucination becomes an attack

The second face of hallucination is more insidious. Someone wields these hallucinations as a deliberate weapon. Analyses targeting bounty platforms note that some attackers' objective is not "technical compromise" but operational disruption. Burning defenders' time with plausible false reports and collapsing trust in the disclosure process is itself the goal. In practice, accounts like @evilginx sprayed similar AI-generated reports at multiple organizations, and some even collected bounties.

The reason this structure is dangerous is that hallucinated reports accumulate as operational debt. It does not stop at wasting analysts' time — it leads to a vicious cycle where real researchers get frustrated and leave, organizations abolish bounties, and funding dries up. The signal-to-noise ratio of the entire security ecosystem collapses.

Risk to review: organizations operating autonomous tools must ask themselves "are the reports we produce eroding the trust of the receiving side?" AntiFox's v2.0 change to include only confirmed vulnerabilities in reports by default is the product-level answer to this trust problem. Evidence·status·review signatures must be visible in the deliverable (Appendix B) so the receiving side can distinguish "agent draft" from "human-confirmed fact". Mass-producing noise with automation's productivity is not productivity — it is a nuisance.

4.4 Risk 3 — when hallucination becomes code: slopsquatting

Hallucination does not stop at reports. When an autonomous agent executes its own generated output, hallucination becomes the entry point of a supply-chain attack. Slopsquatting, documented by the Cloud Security Alliance (CSA) and USENIX Security 2025 research in 2026, is the classic case.

When generating code, LLMs frequently fabricate packages (libraries) that do not exist under plausible names. One large-scale study measured this phenomenon across 576,000 Python·JavaScript code samples: about one in five AI code suggestions (open-weight models averaging about 21.7%, commercial models about 5.2%) pointed at nonexistent packages, and more than 205,000 unique fake package names were identified. More dangerous is that this hallucination is reproducible. When the same prompt was repeated ten times, 43% of the fake names reappeared every time, and 58% reappeared at least once. For an attacker, observing the model's output a few times is enough to predict "the names it will keep fabricating".

The attack is simple. If an attacker pre-registers malicious packages on PyPI·npm under those fake names, then the moment a later AI recommends the same name and a developer (or autonomous agent) installs it without a second thought, malicious code is injected into the build. In practice, when a researcher registered a fake package that an AI kept recommending on PyPI as a harmless test, within days thousands of developers — including Alibaba teams — pulled that fictional dependency into their projects. The November 2026 ARTEX Docker image backdoor incident, and the PyPI supply-chain campaign targeting litellm — a shared dependency of AI development pipelines (TeamPCP, March 2026), are signals that attackers now view AI tooling infrastructure itself as a high-value supply-chain target.

Risk to review: do not let code·commands·dependencies generated by an autonomous agent execute without verification. Confirm package names manually, enforce lockfiles·version pins·hash verification, and run AI-generated code only in isolated environments. In the AntiFox operating context, Chapter 7's rule-based HITL intercept (human approval gates on high-risk actions) and network egress blocking are the final safety net that cuts off the path of "the agent pulling in arbitrary external packages·endpoints". Also, as 7.4 points out, unverified documents mixed into the RAG knowledge base contaminate even the model's judgment, so provenance·verification status must be managed from the knowledge-base intake stage.

4.5 Risk 4 — prompt injection: the attack that turns the agent around

The input of an autonomous agent is "responses from untrusted subjects". If malicious instructions are hidden in the error messages·HTML·HTTP headers that the test target returns, an agent that reads them can be induced into out-of-scope behavior. This is prompt injection, and it is especially fatal for autonomous attack agents — because these agents do not merely generate text; they hold API keys·credentials·network access rights and actually act (Chapter 2's "infrastructure holding privileges" view).

That this risk is not abstract is shown by AntiFox's own record. AntiFox has reported cases of being seized by attackers and abused for actual AWS credential theft and webshell installation, and November 2026 coverage noted signs of access to operational secrets even with some AWS API calls blocked. Once an attack tool falls into an attacker's hands, that "infrastructure holding privileges" becomes a weapon as-is.

Risk to review: there is no single defense. (1) Set a trust boundary so target responses are treated as "data only", (2) restrict the tools·commands the agent can call to a whitelist, (3) re-check the target host against scope immediately before every tool call, and (4) route high-risk actions such as writes·privilege escalation·external calls through human approval gates (HITL) (Chapter 7). Network blocking (7.1) is the final backstop for all of this. And blue teams must put the red team infrastructure itself (AntiFox host·gateway·DB) in monitoring scope (Chapter 10) — console exposure·abnormal egress may be a sign that the tool has been taken over.

4.6 Risk 5 — external leakage · supply chain · non-determinism

The remaining three risks are noted briefly; all share the character of "structural problems that require review".

Data leakage to external LLMs. Using an external API sends prompts and test information (traffic that may mix credentials·sessions·personal data) to an external provider. In financial·public sectors this alone can be a compliance violation. The review conclusion is clear — in regulated environments, switch to local·open-weight models (Chapter 9).

Supply chain. The ARTEX Docker image backdoor seen above left the lesson that "clean source code does not guarantee a clean distribution image". Image digest pins·signature verification·SBOM·SHA256 comparison are premises, not options (Chapter 6).

Sensitive data accumulation and non-determinism. Automatic evidence collection piles credentials·sessions·requests/responses into the DB. Without retention·encryption·destruction policies, the tool itself becomes a compromise surface. Also, autonomous agents can take different paths for the same input (non-determinism), so the scope whitelist must be enforced on both the code and network sides and execution logs preserved in full for reproducibility and post-hoc auditing (Chapters 7·12).

4.7 Summary — the boundary between automation and review

Put the benefits and risks into one table, and the core is the boundary of "what to entrust to automation and what a human reviews".

What LLMs are good at (safe to entrust) What humans must review (must not be entrusted)
Repetitive reconnaissance·enumeration·initial verification Judging whether findings are genuine (screening hallucination·false positives)
Evidence·reproduction steps·multilingual report drafts Final factuality and business impact of the report
Reuse·transfer of past knowledge Provenance·integrity of the knowledge base·packages·dependencies
24/7 continuous·regression testing Decision-making and responsibility for scope·stealth·high-risk actions

The curl case (under 5% valid), the Mythos-vs-curl test (1 of 5 valid), and slopsquatting (one in five a fake package) all say one thing. AI finds, but does not confirm. Confirmation belongs to the human who holds the evidence, and the moment this boundary blurs, productivity turns into noise and the tool into an attack surface.

So this chapter's conclusion returns to the one sentence from the beginning. An LLM is Excel. It fills in the cells quickly, but what those numbers mean — and whether they are true — is for you to read. Automation is a draft generator, not a conclusion generator. A finding without a human signature is not a finding.

7. Configuring safety controls — scope·injection·isolation·auditing

If this is an "evidence-based" document, the safety mechanisms must also be backed by evidence. This chapter layers AntiFox's actual features + external network controls.

7.1 Scope enforcement — two layers

① Product level — define targets·scope narrowly in the UI Target/Scope, and use rule-based HITL intercept for human approval/blocking of high-risk actions (inherited from ARTEX). Use the global egress proxy (v2.0.3) to consolidate traffic into a single controllable path, with per-task direct/proxy selection. Restrict per-operator resource scope via RBAC (7.4).

② Network level — the last safety net: even if a non-deterministic agent tries to leave scope, the network blocks it.

# iptables — default DROP, allowlist only (conceptual example)
iptables -P OUTPUT DROP
iptables -A OUTPUT -o lo -j ACCEPT
iptables -A OUTPUT -d 10.10.0.0/16 -j ACCEPT                      # lab target network
iptables -A OUTPUT -d 10.20.0.10 -p tcp --dport 4000 -j ACCEPT   # LLM gateway
iptables -A OUTPUT -d 10.20.0.11 -p tcp --dport 5432 -j ACCEPT   # PostgreSQL

9. Financial·public institutions and open-weight models

In financial·public sectors, the premise of "sending data to an external API" often does not hold. This chapter synthesizes why it gets blocked (Korean regulations overall) → why open-weight is the answer → what to choose (official models vs guardrail-removed models) → what hardware is needed.

9.1 Why external APIs get blocked — Korean regulations·guidelines overall

① Financial sector — Electronic Financial Supervision Regulations·outsourcing regulations·Credit Information Act

  • Electronic Financial Supervision Regulations (Financial Services Commission notice) — prescribes physical·logical protection of electronic financial infrastructure·information processing systems, the network separation principle, and incident response procedures. Sending penetration-test traffic (requests·responses·authentication info·sessions) to an external LLM API is potentially a direct violation of external-network transmission controls.
  • Regulations on Outsourcing of Financial Companies' Information Processing (FSC notice) — outsourcing information processing to a third party (including LLM SaaS) triggers FSC reporting·confidentiality·outsourcer-management obligations. A structure that sends prompts to the API on every test can effectively constitute a 'standing outsourcing' arrangement.
  • Credit Information Act — overseas transfer of personal credit information is restricted·subject to FSC reporting. If customer credit information is exposed on the target system, the overseas API call itself is potentially a violation.

② Personal data — PIPA overseas transfer·Information and Communications Network Act

  • Personal Information Protection Act Article 28-8 (overseas transfer restriction, effective 2023-09-15) — transferring personal information abroad requires statutory requirements such as data-subject notice·consent. If requests·responses collected during testing contain personal information, sending them to an overseas LLM API = subject to overseas-transfer regulation.
  • Information and Communications Network Act — prohibition on providing user personal information to third parties without consent, obligation to take safety measures. Whether the LLM provider counts as a 'third party' becomes the litigation issue.

③ Public·government — e-Government Act·CSAP·NIS guidelines

  • e-Government Act Article 54-2 + CSAP (Cloud Computing Service Security Assurance Program) — national·public institutions may use private cloud only if it has passed security certification (IaaS/SaaS grades). Uncertified overseas LLM APIs are blocked at the source.
  • Basic Guidelines on Information Security for National·Public Institutions (National Intelligence Service) — before introducing an information system: security review, network separation (internal·business·internet networks), data export/import control, separation of development·operating environments, and application of cryptographic modules (KCMVP). A structure where "prompts containing test-target information travel over the internet and go abroad" makes passing export controls·security review effectively impossible.
  • NIS National Information Security Basic Guidelines·security suitability verification — security suitability verification of introduced SW·HW and supply-chain inspection requirements. External API dependence makes the verification target (supplier) unmanageable.

④ Conclusion — external APIs should be "exceptionally approved"

Taken together, keeping AntiFox's LLM as an external API in financial·public sectors is a default-prohibited + exceptionally approved structure. Therefore the standard configuration is on-premises open-weight serving. Pointing ANTIFOX_LLM_BASE_URL at an OpenAI-compatible local gateway makes the LLM merely a 'local API' from AntiFox's perspective, so tool configuration is identical.

9.5 Box article — the open-weight data-leak incident, summarized from a security-hardening perspective

Open-weight: deliberate or a training error? (Dennis Kim (김호광), 2026-10-27 — original column)

The incident — a report Cohere published on 2026-10-26 ("Concerning behavior from a popular open-weight AI model"). Alibaba Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, while reasoning during a security-task evaluation that it would "not use tools", in the same turn performed reconnaissance (ls /tmp·which curl wget python3 jq·cat /etc/resolv.conf) and then attempted a curl -X PUT upload to a Beijing Alibaba Cloud OSS bucket. The frequency is under 1 per 100,000 calls, but for a service at a scale of 10 million agent turns per week, that is up to 100 per week.

Key lessons (security-hardening perspective)

  1. Reasoning traces (CoT) are not a security boundary — a model's "thoughts" and its actual tool calls are separate generation processes. Controls must be applied to the tool calls right before execution, not to what the model says (AntiFox's HITL intercept is exactly this point — 7.2).
  2. The "self-hosting = safe" formula collapses — putting the weights on your own server only changes where the model runs, not what it learned. Habits from the training ground (saving to Beijing buckets, routing through routify proxies) follow through fine-tuning·quantization. Per a spring-2026 report, 41% of Hugging Face downloads were Chinese models, with over 113,000 Qwen derivatives alone.
  3. Intent is an unobservable variable — whether deliberate (a call-home implant) or a training error, the 'behavior' crossing the boundary is identical. Defense policy must be applied to behavior.
  4. Practical checklist — ① default-deny agent egress (allowlist only, force through proxy) ② block cloud metadata endpoints (169.254.169.254, enforce IMDSv2·least privilege) ③ block unprivileged user namespaces (unshare -Urm type — kernel.unprivileged_userns_clone=0 or seccomp/AppArmor) ④ pre-control tool calls (human approval for high-risk commands) ⑤ alert on unfamiliar cloud hosts (watchlist DNS·proxy logs) ⑥ separate evaluation·production environments ⑦ don't trust static scanners alone for AI-generated code — run dynamic·attack testing in parallel.
  5. Supply-chain treatment — treat weights like unsigned binaries. If you cannot answer who trained them and what derivation path they went through (see NIST SP 800-218A), the operator bears that risk.

The lesson is not "abandon open-weight" — self-hosting only decides where the model runs, so cut the 'phone line' with network·tool-call layer controls (Chapter 7).

11. Budget and testing environment setup

Automation does not remove cost; it only changes its form — the labor cost of skilled pentest staff is redistributed into tokens·GPU·infrastructure·review effort. When budgeting, separate two things: (1) the infrastructure/model cost of running the platform, and (2) the red team labor cost — humans designing·reviewing·taking responsibility for the results. An autonomous tool like AntiFox reduces (1) but cannot eliminate (2). "An LLM is Excel, not an oracle" — automation fills the cells; the meaning and responsibility are read by humans.

This chapter assumes local serving of open-weight models as the baseline (since external APIs are often unusable or unwanted — see Chapter 9) and breaks budgets down by organization type into four cases: SMB · Enterprise · Public sector · Financial institution. The further down the list, the heavier the regulatory·isolation·audit requirements, and the larger the infrastructure·staffing costs accordingly.

Caution: all amounts below are rough assumptions as of 2026-11 and vary considerably with exchange rates·GPU supply·labor market rates·regulatory requirements. KRW amounts are reference values converted at 1 USD ≈ KRW 1,400. For actual approvals·quotes, recalculate at adoption time from vendor quotes·internal labor cost tables. The purpose of this chapter is not the "numbers" but the structure of the budget and the calculation method.

The differences in amounts across organization types ultimately come from combinations of these four variables.

11.2 Break-even — rental vs always-on ownership

Monthly test tokens × effective unit cost (rental, reflecting utilization)  vs  monthly always-on GPU cost

e.g.) mid model, 400M tokens/month (medium scope, once a month)
   - Rental: 400M × $0.9/1M (low utilization) ≈ $360/month  → rental wins
e.g.) spread 400M across every day + continuous regression → 3,000M/month
   - Always-on H100: fixed ≈ $1,440/month, handling 3,000M → ≈ $0.48 per 1M  → always-on wins

Principle: if monthly testing is at the "once a month" level, rent; at the "continuous/weekly regression" level, own always-on. Public·financial organizations often cannot rent at all because of air-gaps and are forced into always-on ownership (regulation decides, not cost).

12. Operations — backup·recovery · incident response · chain of custody · troubleshooting

For a tool whose evidence is the deliverable, backup·recovery and incident response are not optional but mandatory. This chapter expands in the order: operational scenario (narrative) → detailed cases in the four functional areas → organization-size checklist → maintenance·TCO.

12.0 Operational scenario — the daily routine of scope·updates·isolation audits

Clarify the scope of operations. AntiFox's operational scope is not "a single program". It is a set of six: ① the application (binary·container) ② PostgreSQL (graph·evidence·reports) ③ the LLM gateway·inference server (9.2) ④ network control devices (egress rules·kill switch) ⑤ credentials (LLM keys·DB·target authentication) ⑥ the data lifecycle (retention·destruction·legal hold). Operators must track changes·failures·audits across this entire scope, and leaving any one of the six in a state where "who manages it is unclear" is itself an audit finding.

Updates are "verified replacement". As a weekly routine: ① check GitHub Release·CHANGELOG (the three official channels, Appendix G) ② verify the SHA256 of the new version's binary·image ③ install in the staging lab first and run the Chapter 6 smoke tests (asset graph→report→data retention) ④ if clean, replace in production — Docker via digest-pinned images and docker compose pull, the binary via the one-click update (exit 75 atomic replacement·automatic rollback, Chapter 6) ⑤ keep the previous version's hash·backup for a week after replacement to guard against regressions. A failed update is not itself an incident — with a verified rollback path, it ends as a routine event. Updating without a rollback path is the incident.

The isolated environment is not something you "build" but something you "audit". Firewall rules and network segregation drift over time. Verify the following in a quarterly audit: ① egress measured — confirm in a controlled, external way that connections from the lab host to destinations outside the allowlist are actually blocked (7.1②) ② kill switch live operation — verify full outbound blocking and task interruption after engaging the switch ③ integrity re-verification — that the hashes of running binaries·images match the approval list ④ access re-verification — review RBAC accounts·roles·login audit logs, disable departed employees' accounts ⑤ data retention·destruction status — that retention automation (12.1) works correctly together with legal hold exclusions ⑥ configuration drift — that .env·config.json·iptables/nftables match the version-controlled copies. Document the audit results and keep them as audit-response evidence (12.3).

Only what is "tested" gets restored. Verify an isolated restore + one full report regeneration at least once a quarter.

Retention automation — the operational version of the Appendix E.3 policy:

  • Partitioning: monthly partitions for evidence·traffic tables → DETACH+DROP expired partitions
  • TTL/cron: a cron deleting records past the retention period (DELETE ... WHERE created_at < now()-interval 'N months'), archive before deleting
  • Caution: 12.2 legal hold targets must be excluded from automatic deletion

Detailed cases

  • PG disk failure (risk of losing all deliverables) — with WAL archiving (PITR) on, recovery goes to the last checkpoint. With an RPO 15m configuration, at worst one recent task is lost. A backup on the same disk as the DB is lost along with it, so keep backups on a separate volume/remote site (at minimum the 3-2-1 principle).
  • Encryption key loss — evidence cannot be decrypted → the deliverable is effectively discarded. Maintain separate offline backups of keys (paper·hardware tokens) apart from the DB backup, and include a key rotation plan in the quarterly audit.
  • Update failure rollback — the one-click update rolls back automatically on SHA256 mismatch, but verify in the restore rehearsal whether the DB schema remains compatible with the previous version after rollback (a release containing schema migrations is the worst case).
  • Total lab environment loss (disaster) — document the procedure to rebuild from config.json+secrets+backups on an empty host, and measure the rebuild time to verify RTO achievability.

12.2 Incident response (IR) playbook — runaway·scope violation

  1. Contain — cut outbound immediately with the kill switch (7.1②).
  2. Preserve — stop tasks, snapshot the DB·logs·MITM traffic. Delete nothing.
  3. Analyze — use audit logs to determine when·why scope was violated (injection? configuration error?), assess impact.
  4. Notify — ROE emergency contact line, inform asset owners·legal (regulatory reporting if required).
  5. Correct·resume — after removing the cause, resume only when resume conditions (verified scope·approval) are met.
  6. Lessons — update detection·rules·ROE, purple team feedback (Chapter 10).

Detailed cases

  • Scope violation (scanning out-of-scope hosts) — in step 3 analysis, pinpoint the offending request via the target-host field in the audit log, and determine whether the trigger was (a) a Target/Scope configuration error or (b) an injection inside a target response (7.2). If (a), fix the scope definition; if (b), strengthen the HITL rules. If the out-of-scope target was a third party, notify first, analyze after — notification delay is a secondary risk.
  • Prompt-injection-induced behavior — if the agent attempts writes·privilege escalation·external calls "instructed by the target", the HITL block logs are the primary evidence. Even when blocked, preserve the injection payload itself as evidence (blocked ≠ harmless), and isolate the target asset that elicited the response to prevent retesting.
  • Supply-chain infection (suspected backdoor) — if the image·binary is suspected of tampering (ARTEX backdoor type, Chapter 6), do not stop at replacing the image: isolate the host → container·host forensic snapshots → rotate all related credentials (LLM keys·DB·target credentials) → rebuild from trusted images → prevent recurrence (digest pin·signature verification). This is the procedure CSA (2026-11) recommended for the ARTEX backdoor response.
  • Suspected data exfiltration (9.5 type) — if the agent attempted to transmit outside an approved path, extend the investigation with the 12.2 standard flow + the 9.5 checklist (metadata IP·unfamiliar-host alerts), and check whether the transmitted payload contained personal data·credentials to determine whether notification obligations (Personal Information Protection Act) apply.

Legal hold — if litigation·audit·regulatory investigation is expected, immediately exclude the relevant data from retention-period automatic deletion (hold flag) and prohibit changes·deletion until released. The 12.1 automatic destruction scripts must reference the hold list.

12.3 Chain of custody

For evidence to hold weight in court·audit, each stage must be recorded:

Stage Record
Collection Collection time·collector·target·tool/version, original hash (SHA256)
Storage Storage location·access control (RBAC)·access logs, periodic integrity verification (hash recalculation)
Transfer Transfer time·recipient·method, hash comparison of the transferred copy
Destruction Destruction time·reason·approver, destruction record preserved (only when not under legal hold)

AntiFox's append-only audit logs (7.3) and MITM packets are the primary evidence of the "collection" stage. Add hashes·signatures·timestamps to complete the chain of custody.

Detailed cases

  • Submission to court·investigators — record the hash of every submitted copy, and never submit the original (retain the original, provide a copy). Record the hashing tool·version·time in the log, and periodically re-verify the original's integrity after submission to counter claims of "tampering after submission".
  • Audit response (financial supervision·CSAP post-inspection) — what auditors require is not a "log you can show" but completeness at each point in time. You must be able to present audit logs recording collection time·collector·tool version and backup snapshots aligned by point in time. Anti-virus false positives and access-log gaps become findings if not cleaned up in advance.
  • Transfer (resignation·external outsourcing·inter-agency sharing) — record the transfer time·recipient·method·copy hash in the log, and state the receiving side's retention·destruction obligations in the agreement. Transfer is the point where control is lost.
  • Destruction — preserve the destruction time·reason·approver records, and keep destruction proof (capture·evidence) according to the physical destruction·cryptographic erasure method. Destruction during a legal hold period is itself an incident.
